# Visual system

This system is an abstraction from four reference plogs. It captures recurring design choices without retaining or reproducing the source images.

## Core impression

The look sits between a personal food diary, an editorial menu, and a handmade scrapbook. Real food gives credibility and appetite appeal; hand-drawn marks add intimacy. The page should feel composed by a person with taste, not generated from a rigid template.

## Palette

Use one background family per page.

### Warm dark / atmospheric

- cacao brown `#2D261F`
- charcoal brown `#3C352B`
- warm taupe `#5E5851`
- muted wood `#87634A`
- parchment `#E4DCCE`
- chalk white `#F7F3E8`

Use for restaurant interiors, evening meals, coffee, fusion food, and moody travel diaries. Darken a real background slightly so food cutouts and light lettering stay legible.

### Sunny paper / menu

- butter yellow `#F7E077`
- soft custard `#F9E37C`
- warm cream `#F2E7C9`
- ink brown `#3B312A`
- muted ceramic blue `#315A8E`

Use for breakfast, picnic, fruit, bakery, summer, and playful menus.

### Accent rule

Let the food supply saturated accents. Add no more than one designed accent family: ochre `#E7A91E`, coral `#C85B45`, leaf green `#667A3A`, or ceramic blue `#315A8E`. A useful balance is 60–75% ground, 20–35% food/natural color, and 5–10% lettering/outlines/doodles.

Avoid pure black expanses, sterile pure white, candy gradients, cyber neon, cool corporate blue, or more than two unrelated accent hues.

## Material and image treatment

- Food stays photorealistic, with believable moisture, crumbs, char, glaze, ceramic, glass, and cut edges.
- Lighting is warm natural window light or soft restaurant ambient light. Keep gentle shadows so cutouts do not look flat.
- Finish is matte with subtle grain and slightly softened contrast. Avoid plastic gloss, hyper-HDR, or luxury-ad perfection.
- Mix clean cutouts with intentionally imperfect 3–6 px hand-drawn outlines on a roughly 1000 px-wide canvas. White/chalk outlines suit dark pages; ochre suits warm interiors; dark ink suits yellow paper.
- A light offset shadow is optional. Do not use heavy stickers, thick drop shadows, bevels, or uniform vector borders.

## Composition

Default to a portrait `3:4` or `4:5` canvas. Keep a 5–8% safe margin.

### Shared geometry

- Establish one obvious hero occupying about 25–40% of the canvas.
- Use 2–6 supporting food or ingredient clusters at varied sizes.
- Create a loose diagonal, S-curve, or triangular reading path; do not snap everything to a grid.
- Reserve 20–35% as quiet space. Empty space may be a flat color, a dim wall, sky, tabletop, or uncluttered interior.
- Pair a dense zone with a calm zone. Balance large food masses with small annotations rather than centering every object.
- Allow mild overlap but never cover the food's defining features or make labels collide.

### Hero cover

Place one dish off-center, usually near the upper-middle or central third. Let the real background establish place and mood. Use 2–5 small doodles around the hero and a compact title in the lower or side quiet zone. Do not float a dish at exactly the same scale and position every time.

### Journal collage

Use irregular cutouts at different rotations and scales. One hero anchors the page; supporting items orbit it along a diagonal or S-curve. Alternate image clusters and handwritten notes. A full-bleed, slightly darkened background can act as the page texture.

### Sunny menu

Use a flat butter-yellow/cream paper field and top-down plated foods with natural shadows. Set the title large in one corner and distribute 5–8 foods like a loose editorial flat-lay. Keep gaps uneven but intentional.

## Typography

- Primary voice: naïve hand lettering with visible stroke variation, slightly irregular baseline, and airy tracking. It should feel friendly and editorial, not childish bubble lettering or formal calligraphy.
- Secondary voice: lighter handwritten notes or a soft typewriter/slab face for a cover caption.
- Use at most two voices and two weights.
- Title scale: roughly 7–13% of canvas height. Annotation scale: roughly 2–3.5%.
- On dark imagery, use chalk white or cream. On yellow/cream, use ink brown rather than absolute black.
- English may use irregular uppercase marker lettering plus a smaller cursive phrase. Chinese should remain simple, relaxed, and highly legible.
- Avoid dense body copy, perfectly geometric sans-serif systems, metallic 3D type, fake brush calligraphy, and centered corporate poster layouts.

## Doodle vocabulary

Use 3–8 marks total, selected for the theme: four-point stars, tiny hearts, ingredient silhouettes, steam curls, crumbs, sparkle dots, arrows, speech bubbles, mushrooms, leaves, utensils, or a curved underline. Draw them as quick monoline gestures with uneven edges.

Invent a fresh set for each topic. Do not reuse the same star placement, mascot, speech bubble, or decorative cluster from a reference. Emoji can inspire the idea but should be redrawn in the same handmade line language.

## Copy tone

Write like a compact personal tasting note: specific, sensory, lightly playful. Mention contrasts such as crispy/soft, tart/sweet, smoky/fresh, hot/cold, or rich/clean. Prefer observations over generic praise. Avoid excessive exclamation marks, influencer clichés, and unverified superlatives.

## Originality guardrails

Do not reproduce a reference's exact food selection, background photo, plate positions, title phrase, crop, visual mascot, or line-by-line annotation layout. Do not include the reference images as reusable assets. Every output must introduce a new content hierarchy and theme-specific doodles while preserving the higher-level palette, tactile collage method, spacing, and typographic temperament.
